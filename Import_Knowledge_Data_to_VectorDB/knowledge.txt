Corgi Dog Facts

Corgis are a small herding dog breed originating from Wales, with two main types: the
Pembroke Welsh Corgi and the Cardigan Welsh Corgi. They are easily recognized by their
long, low bodies, short legs, large upright ears, and fox-like faces. Pembrokes typically
have short or docked tails, while Cardigans keep a long, fluffy tail. Their double coat
comes in shades of red, sable, fawn, and black-and-tan, often with white markings on the
legs, chest, and face. Despite their short stature, corgis have a sturdy, muscular build
that reflects their history as working cattle-herding dogs.

Both corgi breeds share a similar general shape, but they are actually genetically
distinct rather than simply variants of one breed. The Cardigan Welsh Corgi is considered
the older of the two, believed to have been brought to Wales by Celtic tribes thousands of
years ago, while the Pembroke Welsh Corgi is thought to have Scandinavian roots, possibly
linked to dogs brought by Vikings or Flemish weavers. Adult corgis typically stand around
10 to 12 inches tall at the shoulder and weigh between 24 and 30 pounds, making them small
enough for apartment living yet solidly built enough to have once herded cattle many times
their size by nipping at their heels and dodging kicks.

In terms of diet, corgis do best on high-quality dog food appropriate for their size and
activity level, whether that's a well-balanced commercial kibble, wet food, or a vet-
approved raw or homemade diet. Because they have a tendency to gain weight easily, portion
control is especially important; treats should be given sparingly, and foods like
chocolate, grapes, onions, and xylitol (a common sweetener) must be strictly avoided as
they are toxic to dogs. Corgis are notorious for their hearty appetites and persistent
begging, so owners often need to be firm about feeding schedules to prevent overeating.

Most adult corgis do well on two measured meals a day rather than free feeding, since
leaving food out around the clock makes it far too easy for them to overeat. Puppies
generally need three to four smaller meals daily to support healthy growth, gradually
transitioning to an adult schedule by around one year of age. Fresh water should always be
available, and owners are often advised to use a slow-feeder bowl or puzzle feeder, both
to slow down fast eaters and to provide the mental stimulation this intelligent breed
craves.

Behaviorally, corgis are intelligent, energetic, and highly trainable, a legacy of their
herding background, though this same instinct can lead them to nip at heels or chase
moving objects like children, bikes, or other pets. They are alert and vocal, often
barking to announce visitors or express excitement, and they thrive on mental stimulation
and regular exercise. Corgis tend to bond closely with their families and can become quite
attached, sometimes developing separation anxiety if left alone for long periods. Early
socialization and consistent training help channel their herding instincts and vocal
tendencies into more manageable behaviors, and they generally respond very well to
positive reinforcement methods.

Health-wise, their long backs and short legs make them prone to intervertebral disc
disease (IVDD) and other spinal problems, so jumping from heights, rough play on stairs,
and excess weight should be avoided to protect their spine. They are also predisposed to
obesity, hip dysplasia, degenerative myelopathy, and eye conditions like progressive
retinal atrophy. Treating these issues often involves weight management, controlled
exercise, orthopedic support, physical therapy, and in more serious cases, surgery or
long-term medication. Because many of these conditions are progressive or breed-linked,
preventive care, maintaining a healthy weight, and regular veterinary checkups are
essential for catching problems early and keeping a corgi comfortable and mobile
throughout its typical 12 to 15 year lifespan.
